A Quebec Superior Court has issued a temporary ban on two men, a father and his son, from donating sperm after allegations surfaced that they have collectively fathered hundreds of children, raising serious legal, ethical, and public health concerns. The injunction, issued by Justice Simon Chamberland, prevents both individuals from continuing sperm donations while the broader legal case unfolds. The decision follows a lawsuit filed by a woman who conceived four children using their genetic material, three with the father and one with the son. The plaintiff claims there was an understanding that the men would limit the number of children conceived through their donations. According to her, one was expected to stop after 25 children and the other after 10. However, she later discovered through her own investigation that the numbers may be vastly higher. Scale of donations raises alarm Court records indicate that one of the men may be linked to more than 450 children, while the other could be connected to over 160. Although the defendants deny agreeing to any fixed limits, they acknowledge fathering more children than what the plaintiff alleges was discussed. Justice Chamberland noted that these figures significantly exceed what experts consider safe or responsible in sperm donation practices. Typically, limits are recommended to reduce the likelihood of biological relatives unknowingly forming relationships and to manage potential genetic risks . The court emphasized that each additional donation could increase potential harm, not only to the plaintiff and her children but also to other families unknowingly connected through the same donors. The risks include accidental consanguinity and the spread of hereditary conditions . The ruling highlights how such situations can create long-term complications for donor-conceived children, particularly when accurate records or transparency are lacking. This case underscores the challenges of regulating sperm donation outside formal medical systems. Informal arrangements, often made privately, can bypass safeguards such as tracking, screening, and limits on offspring.
