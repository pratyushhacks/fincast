A fresh week of economic data is set to test investor sentiment in the US, with the upcoming employment report taking center stage even as geopolitical tensions continue to dominate market direction. According to Reuters, investors are closely monitoring developments in the ongoing Iran conflict, now entering its second month. The prolonged tensions in the Middle East have significantly disrupted oil supplies, pushing U.S. crude prices up more than 70% so far this year to around $100 per barrel. The sharp rise has filtered through to consumers, with gasoline prices climbing to roughly $4 per gallon—raising concerns about a potential squeeze on household spending. At the same time, concerns about inflation have resurfaced. Benchmark U.S. Treasury yields have surged to their highest levels since last summer, creating a new pressure point for equity valuations. Higher yields tend to reduce the attractiveness of stocks by increasing borrowing costs and tightening financial conditions. Equity markets have already shown signs of strain. As per Reuters, the S&P 500 has declined for five consecutive weeks and is down more than 7% since late February, when U.S.-Israeli military strikes on Iran escalated tensions. The Nasdaq Composite and Dow Jones Industrial Average have also slipped into correction territory, each falling over 10% from their record highs. Market movements have remained highly sensitive to headlines, with shifting signals around possible de-escalation causing volatility across asset classes. The final days of the first quarter have been particularly challenging for investors. Beyond geopolitical risks, concerns around disruptions from artificial intelligence and stress in the private credit market have also weighed on sentiment. The S&P 500 is down roughly 7% so far in 2026, following three years of strong double-digit gains, underscoring the sharp shift in market momentum, Reuters said. The March employment report, due this week, will be a crucial data point. Reuters data suggests the U.S. economy is expected to have added around 55,000 jobs, with the unemployment rate projected at 4.4%. This comes after a surprisingly weak February report that showed a loss of 92,000 jobs. With recent data indicating fragility in the labor market, even a modest positive reading could help stabilise investor confidence. Additional economic indicators—including retail sales and manufacturing and services activity—are also scheduled for release, offering a broader view of economic health. The Federal Reserve faces a complex backdrop. While concerns about a weakening labor market prompted rate cuts last year, persistently high inflation—now exacerbated by rising energy prices—limits the central bank’s flexibility. According to Reuters, markets are currently pricing in no further rate cuts this year, with some expectations even shifting toward a possible rate hike in 2026. Meanwhile, bond markets are sending their own signals. The 10-year Treasury yield has climbed above 4.4%, up from around 4% before the conflict began. Rising yields have wide-ranging implications, from mortgage rates to government borrowing costs, and play a key role in determining equity valuations. Reflecting this shift, the S&P 500’s price-to-earnings ratio has moderated in recent weeks, falling below 20 from over 22 at the start of the year, according to Reuters-cited data. However, valuations remain elevated compared to the long-term average of around 16, suggesting limited room for complacency. Investors are also assessing the impact of higher energy costs on corporate earnings. While some companies have managed to reassure markets despite cost pressures, the broader earnings season—set to gather pace in the coming weeks—will be critical in shaping expectations. Overall, markets appear caught between resilient economic fundamentals and rising macroeconomic risks. With geopolitical developments, interest rates, and labor market data all in play, volatility is likely to remain a defining feature in the near term.
