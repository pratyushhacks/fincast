The excise duty cut is aimed at cushioning consumers from a potential sharp spike in fuel prices, rather than making fuel cheaper. The Centre’s ₹10 per litre excise duty cut on petrol and diesel may appear to offer relief, but consumers are unlikely to see any immediate reduction in pump prices as the benefit is set to be absorbed by oil marketing companies amid a sharp surge in global crude, according to The Economic Time s. The tax on petrol has been reduced to ₹3 per litre, while diesel has become zero at the central excise level. Why the government took this step The decision comes amid a sharp surge in global crude oil prices , triggered by geopolitical tensions in West Asia and disruptions in key supply routes like the Strait of Hormuz. Crude prices have jumped dramatically from around $70 per barrel to over $120 putting massive pressure on India, which imports most of its oil. To prevent a sudden spike in retail fuel prices, the government chose to cut its own tax revenue and absorb part of the shock. Why petrol and diesel prices may not fall Despite the ₹10 per litre excise duty cut , consumers may not see an immediate reduction in fuel prices due to multiple offsetting factors. State-run oil firms such as IOC, BPCL and HPCL are incurring losses of ₹24–30 per litre amid high crude prices. As a result, the benefit from the excise duty cut is expected to be absorbed by these companies to partly offset their losses, rather than being passed on to consumers. Global crude oil prices have surged sharply, rising by 30–50 per cent in several regions amid ongoing geopolitical tensions and supply disruptions. This increase in input costs has effectively neutralised the impact of tax reduction, leaving little room for any meaningful decline in retail fuel prices. Petrol and diesel prices are not directly controlled by the government. Instead, oil marketing companies determine them based on international crude prices, exchange rate movements and their own cost structures. This means that even when taxes are reduced, companies are not obligated to immediately lower pump prices. The excise duty cut is aimed at cushioning consumers from a potential sharp spike in fuel prices, rather than making fuel cheaper. Without this intervention, prices at the pump could have risen significantly in response to the global oil rally. Market uncertainty persists Ongoing stress in global energy markets, driven by geopolitical tensions and concerns over supply routes, continues to create uncertainty. In such a scenario, oil companies are likely to prioritise financial stability over price cuts, further reducing the chances of immediate relief for consumers. The excise duty cut looks like relief, but in reality, it is a protective measure, not a price-cut measure. Consumers should not expect cheaper fuel right now. Instead, the move is helping stabilise prices during a global oil crisis, even if that means the benefit does not show up at the petrol pump immediately.
