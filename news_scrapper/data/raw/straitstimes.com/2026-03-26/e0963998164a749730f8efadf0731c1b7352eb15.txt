When missiles and drones began striking the United Arab Emirates amid the widening confrontation between the United States, Israel and Iran, Abu Dhabi faced a scenario it had long sought to avoid: becoming a direct target in a regional war . For a country built on stability, connectivity and investor confidence, the strikes were a significant stress test. Yet the more revealing question is not whether the UAE avoided damage but how it continued to function with limited disruption – despite being among the most frequently targeted states in the Gulf during this phase of the conflict, alongside Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar and, to a lesser extent, eastern Saudi Arabia. The answer lies in three factors: layered air defence, institutionalised crisis management, and infrastructure designed for rapid recovery. While the UAE did not achieve invulnerability, it demonstrated an ability to absorb shocks while maintaining operational continuity across critical sectors. The UAE came under some of the heaviest fire since the Iran conflict erupted, facing the largest reported numbers of Iranian projectiles and repeated strikes on military, aviation and energy infrastructure among Gulf states. For weeks, debris from intercepted missiles caused fires at energy-related facilities, including near storage and logistics zones, while at least one drone strike near Dubai International Airport led to temporary flight disruptions. Ports such as Jebel Ali experienced brief interruptions due to falling debris, and residents in major cities reported hearing regular interception activity overhead. Despite this, government services, financial markets, logistics hubs and most businesses continued operating, with only short-lived interruptions. Casualties remained limited – eight reported deaths and 150 injured – despite the scale of attacks. PHOTO: REUTERS The centrepiece of the UAE’s response was a multi-layered air and missile defence system built over more than a decade, designed specifically to counter the Gulf’s evolving threat environment of ballistic missiles, cruise missiles and drones. Official data released during the crisis provides a clearer empirical basis for assessing performance. By early March, the UAE authorities reported detecting approximately 186 ballistic missiles, eight cruise missiles and over 800 drones. Of these, roughly 172 ballistic missiles and 755 drones were successfully intercepted, alongside all reported cruise missiles – implying interception rates exceeding 90 per cent for the most dangerous threats, with somewhat lower but still substantial rates for drones. Even allowing for the optimism of official data, the performance was high by contemporary standards. The ballistic missile defence layer carried the heaviest burden, neutralising the most destructive category of threats before impact. This outcome reflects a tiered defence architecture. At the upper tier, the Terminal High Altitude Area Defence (THAAD) system intercepts ballistic missiles in their terminal phase, preventing catastrophic strikes on urban centres and strategic infrastructure. Beneath it, US Patriot systems, supplemented by South Korea’s KM-SAM Cheongung-II surface-to-air missile system, provide a secondary interception layer for missiles that evade the upper tier. These are complemented by shorter-range systems, radar networks and electronic warfare capabilities tasked with countering drones, including systems such as the Russian Pantsir-S1 surface-to-air anti-aircraft system. In practice, this layering created redundancy and avoided dangerous reliance on any single system. No layer is expected to be perfect; each compensates for the others. The approach also reflects the reality of modern conflict, which increasingly involves mixed salvos – ballistic missiles, cruise missiles and drones launched simultaneously to overwhelm defences. PHOTO: AFP The origins of this architecture lie in a series of strategic decisions shaped by the UAE’s threat perception over two decades. From the mid-2000s, in response to Iran’s expanding missile capabilities, particularly the development of Shahab and Sejjil-class missiles capable of reaching Gulf targets, and persistent tensions in the Strait of Hormuz given repeated Iranian threats to disrupt shipping, the UAE invested heavily in missile defence. It became the first country outside the United States to acquire THAAD in the 2010s. Subsequent conflict reinforced this defence strategy including the increasing sophistication of Hezbollah’s rocket warfare , and later the proliferation of missile and drone capabilities among non-state actors. The Yemen conflict in the mid-2010s , and in particular the Houthi missile and drone attacks on Abu Dhabi in 2022, shaped the UAE’s threat perceptions and reinforced the immediacy of the threat. In response, the UAE expanded procurement of advanced interceptors and radar systems, accelerated integration with US-linked defence networks and enhanced investments in counter-drone technologies and early-warning systems. This included the multibillion-dollar acquisition of South Korea’s KM-SAM Cheongung-II in 2022, valued at approximately US$3.5 billion (S$4.5 billion), as well as additional Patriot interceptor purchases and upgrades to radar and command-and-control networks. The UAE also moved beyond reliance on Western suppliers, deepening ties with South Korea and Turkey, in areas such as drones, air defence technologies and electronic warfare. Diversified procurement reduces vulnerability to supply disruptions, political constraints or wartime shortages. South Korean partnerships have contributed to missile defence integration and radar capabilities, while Turkish cooperation in drone and counter-drone technologies has strengthened the UAE’s ability to address lower-tier aerial threats, capabilities that proved particularly relevant in intercepting and mitigating drone swarms during the current conflict. In parallel, the UAE invested in domestic production through entities such as , which have secured multibillion-dollar defence contracts with the UAE Armed Forces in precision-guided munitions to counter-drone solutions, and enabled domestically produced capabilities during periods of heightened tension. Taken together, these investments reflect a broader strategic approach: building not just a collection of systems, but a resilient and adaptable defence ecosystem to respond to a wide spectrum of threats. Despite high interception rates, the UAE still experienced visible incidents, including fires at energy-related facilities, debris impacts near infrastructure zones and temporary disruptions to aviation operations. This reflects a fundamental characteristic of modern air defence: successful interception does not eliminate physical consequences, as debris from destroyed projectiles can still cause damage on the ground. Government briefings indicated that a significant portion of recorded impacts resulted from interception debris rather than direct missile strikes, highlighting the limits of even highly effective defensive systems. Yet such incidents did not cripple the UAE. At the Jebel Ali port, a fire triggered by falling debris was contained within hours, with operations resuming the same day. Similarly, following a drone-related incident near Dubai International Airport, aviation authorities temporarily suspended flights before restoring services in phases , allowing the airport to maintain its function as a global transit hub. This points to a second, less visible layer of defence: systems designed for rapid recovery, which includes pre-existing contingency planning, redundancy in infrastructure, and regular national crisis simulations to test crisis protocols among government agencies, port operators, airlines and emergency services, allowing disruptions to be managed without cascading effects across the wider economy. National frameworks led by the UAE’s National Emergency Crisis and Disaster Management Authority enabled coordinated responses across government and the private sector. Across the country, adaptive measures were implemented to sustain normal activity. Schools and universities transitioned to distance learning, while parts of the private sector adopted remote work arrangements, reducing exposure without halting economic activity. The UAE’s ability to sustain operations under sustained attack also reflects the role of centralised crisis governance. Drawing on its Covid-19 experience, the UAE deployed a whole-of-government model integrating civil defence, infrastructure management and public communication. The authorities also moved quickly against misinformation, including AI-generated videos of explosions and strikes, to prevent public panic. UAE officials’ emphasis that the country remained open for business and committed to its global hub strategy also sought to arrest a collapse of investor confidence and shore up support for impacted businesses and ongoing projects. While the Dubai and Abu Dhabi stock exchanges were closed for two days to avoid a disorderly sell-off, in contrast to initial fears of large-scale capital flight and an expatriate exodus, the market turmoil has been brief and contained. In all, three lessons from the UAE’s experience stand out. First, layered air defence systems are essential but insufficient on their own. Second, residual damage is unavoidable and must be planned for. Third, resilience depends on the speed and visibility of recovery. The UAE’s experience suggests that modern security is defined less by impermeability and more by the ability to keep the economy going despite such hits. The UAE did not achieve invulnerability but accomplished a more realistic objective: the ability to absorb repeated shocks while avoiding systemic breakdown. In an era of drone swarms and missile saturation, that may be the more meaningful benchmark of success. He is also an adviser at Gulf States Analytics, a Washington-based geopolitical risk consultancy.
